# Syracuse University Athletics Communications  
## Synthetic Media Use Policy

**Document type:** Internal policy (effective upon adoption)  
**Applies to:** Athletics Communications staff, student workers, contractors, and any person producing official or semi-official athletics media for Syracuse University Athletics  
**Owner:** Associate Athletics Director for Communications (or designee)  
**Background:** Informed by hands-on synthetic audio work documented in [Task_06_Deep_Fake](https://github.com/Avish16/Task_06_Deep_Fake)

---

### 1. Purpose

Synthetic media tools (text-to-speech, avatar video, voice cloning, generative imagery used as “speaking” content) can speed production. They can also impersonate coaches, student-athletes, and university officials, or circulate false claims in a trusted voice.

This policy sets **clear yes/no boundaries** for Athletics Communications so staff can decide Monday morning whether a proposed use is allowed, needs review, or is refused.

---

### 2. Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Synthetic media** | Audio, video, or audiovisual content where a voice or likeness is generated or significantly altered by AI so that a listener/viewer may treat it as a person speaking or appearing |
| **Premade / generic voice** | A vendor voice not trained to imitate a specific living person affiliated with Syracuse |
| **Voice or likeness clone** | Any model or workflow intended to sound or look like a specific identifiable person |
| **Official channel** | University-controlled sites, social accounts, podcasts, email, SMS, or media distributed as Syracuse Athletics content |
| **Disclosure** | Clear notice that content is AI-generated, present in the media itself and in accompanying text |

---

### 3. Permitted uses

The following are **allowed** only when all conditions in Section 5–7 are met:

1. **Accessibility drafts** — Premade-voice TTS of *already approved* written copy (press releases, game notes) for internal review or accessibility prototyping, not for public release unless separately approved.
2. **Labeled explanatory audio** — Short, disclosed, premade-voice readings of **verified** public statistics or approved written summaries, clearly framed as synthetic narration, never as a coach or AD speaking.
3. **Generic B-roll / stock-style generative imagery** — Non-identifiable backgrounds or illustrations that do not depict real SU athletes, coaches, or staff.
4. **Training and research demos** — Internal examples used to teach staff what synthetic media sounds like, stored only on university systems, labeled synthetic, not posted publicly without review.

If a proposed use is not on this list, treat it as **prohibited or review-required** (Section 4 and 8), not as “probably fine with a caption.”

---

### 4. Prohibited uses

These are **refused outright**, regardless of disclosure, intent, or speed:

1. **Impersonation** — Any clone or imitation of a coach, student-athlete, athletics staff member, donor, recruit, opponent, official, or other identifiable person without a documented consent workflow (Section 5). “We’ll label it AI” does not authorize impersonation.
2. **Fabricated quotes or events** — Synthetic audio/video asserting statements, injuries, transfers, discipline, NIL deals, academic status, or game outcomes that are false or unverified.
3. **Recruiting deception** — Personalized synthetic messages that imply a specific coach is speaking to a prospect or family, or that overpromise aid, playing time, or roster spots.
4. **Crisis or emergency communications** — No synthetic voice/face for weather closures, safety alerts, medical updates, or misconduct statements.
5. **Undisclosed public release** — Publishing synthetic speech or talking-head content on official channels without required disclosure (Section 6).
6. **Opponent or third-party deepfakes** — Any synthetic depiction of opponents, officials, or fans used to mock, provoke, or mislead.
7. **High-volume unreviewed generation** — Bulk generation of personalized synthetic voice messages without per-batch approval by the policy owner (see Scale controls in Section 8).

---

### 5. Consent workflow

When a **real person’s** voice, face, or clearly identifiable mannerisms would be used:

1. **Who obtains consent:** The project lead; student workers may not obtain consent alone for coaches or student-athletes.
2. **What consent covers:** Medium (audio/video), purpose, channels, duration, and whether derivatives (clips, ads) are allowed.
3. **Form:** Written (email sufficient if it states the above); store in the project folder with the asset.
4. **Validity:** Maximum **12 months** or end of the stated campaign, whichever is shorter; renew for reuse.
5. **Revocation:** The person may revoke in writing; Communications must stop new distribution within **2 business days** and remove content from official channels it controls as soon as practicable.
6. **Minors / prospects:** Do not create synthetic likenesses of recruits or minors for athletics communications under this policy.

**Default:** Prefer premade voices and non-identifiable visuals so consent is not required. If you need a specific person, get a real recording when possible.

---

### 6. Disclosure standards

For every public or semi-public synthetic media asset:

1. **In-media disclosure** — Spoken (audio) or on-screen (video) statement that the content is AI-generated, near the start; for clips longer than 60 seconds, repeat or keep a persistent on-screen label.
2. **Caption / post text** — Explicit “AI-generated” or “synthetic audio/video” language on the platform post.
3. **Filename / asset title** — Prefix `SYNTHETIC_` (or equivalent) in internal storage.
4. **No reliance on metadata alone** — Assume re-shares strip metadata; disclosure must survive a mid-clip listen/view where feasible.
5. **Excerpts** — If Communications itself publishes an excerpt, the excerpt must still carry disclosure.

---

### 7. Provenance requirements

1. Keep the **final script**, **tool name/version**, **voice ID**, **date**, and **approver** with the asset.
2. Where the vendor supports content credentials or export watermarks, **enable them**; do not strip them before archive.
3. Do not treat provenance as a substitute for disclosure or truth verification.

---

### 8. Review and approval

| Use type | Required approval |
| --- | --- |
| Internal accessibility draft (premade TTS) | Desk supervisor |
| Public disclosed premade-voice narration | Policy owner or designated editor |
| Any use of a real person’s voice/likeness | Policy owner **and** documented consent |
| Anything involving recruiting, medical, discipline, or crisis themes | **Prohibited** or escalated to Athletics leadership — default refuse |

**Review standard:** (1) Is content verified? (2) Is anyone impersonated? (3) Is disclosure durable? (4) What happens if the middle is clipped and reshared? (5) Is volume low enough to review?

**Scale control:** More than **five** synthetic voice assets for a single campaign requires written batch approval and a named reviewer who spot-checks every asset before publish.

---

### 9. Incident response

If synthetic content is misattributed, stripped of disclosure, or used to spread a false claim:

1. **Notify** the policy owner same day.  
2. **Correct** on official channels with a clear statement of what was synthetic and what is true.  
3. **Preserve** the original file, script, and approval trail.  
4. **Request takedown** from platforms where possible.  
5. **Debrief** within five business days: what control failed, policy update needed?

---

### 10. Refusal — when we do not produce synthetic media at all

Communications will **decline** synthetic production — even if someone asks for “just a quick AI voiceover” — when:

- The request requires sounding like a specific coach, athlete, or official without consent and a real recording is available instead.  
- The content cannot be verified before publish.  
- The use is recruiting-facing personalization, crisis communication, or disciplinary messaging.  
- Timeline pressure would skip review.  
- Leadership judges reputational risk higher than any production benefit.

Staff will not be penalized for refusing a request that violates this policy.

---

### 11. Compliance

Violations may result in loss of tool access, removal of content, and referral under university employment or student conduct rules. Contractors must follow this policy as a condition of work.

---

## Limitations and residual risk

This section is part of the policy package. It states what the document **cannot** guarantee.

### Where good-faith staff still fail

- A careful producer labels a full file; a fan **clips the middle** and the label is gone. The policy requires durable disclosure but cannot control every re-encode.  
- Staff may treat “premade voice + caption” as enough when the **script itself** overclaims. Reviewers can miss soft promises in recruiting-adjacent copy.  
- **Batch pressure** (five-asset rule) may still be gamed by splitting campaigns.

### Where bad-faith actors are not stopped

- This policy binds Athletics Communications. It does **not** stop outsiders from cloning a coach from public pressers. Incident response and platform takedowns are partial remedies.  
- A determined insider who ignores the policy can still generate prohibited content on a personal account; employment consequences are after-the-fact.

### Reliance on good faith

Yes — the policy assumes most staff want to protect athletes and the university. That is honest, not fatal: **hard prohibitions** (no impersonation, no fabricated claims, no crisis TTS) reduce the space where good faith is the only control. Soft areas (accessibility drafts, labeled stats narration) still need culture and spot audits.

### Residual risk (summary)

Listeners may still trust synthetic speech. Detectors and watermarks remain incomplete. Disclosure travels poorly. The policy lowers institutional risk for *official* production; it does not eliminate synthetic-media risk in the athletics information environment.

**Bottom line:** Adopt this policy to constrain what *we* make and how we respond — not to claim the internet is safe.
